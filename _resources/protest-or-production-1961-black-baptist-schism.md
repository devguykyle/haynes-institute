# Protest or Production: The 1961 Black Baptist Schism

Many young Black Americans look around today and find themselves asking difficult questions about our culture and heritage as a people. Our cities struggle with disorder. Our churches are often theologically weak. Family instability, economic dependency, and cultural confusion have become familiar realities. Meanwhile, we are told that the Civil Rights era represents the unquestioned triumph of Black America. Yet, we cannot help but reject the idea that the path we inherited has produced the fruit we were promised.

More importantly, we are left wondering whether anyone saw these dangers coming and if the movement could rightly be called good.

Of course, we want to argue that many good things have come from the civil rights movement, but it also seems like so much moral value was abandoned for "social progress" that the trade off appears to be hardly worth it.

Even asking these kinds of questions can feel internally alarming since it could implicate us as abandoning our race and even hating ourselves.

The surprising resolution that many Black Americans are finding to this issue is that the Civil Rights Movement as perpetrated by MLK and the mores attached to it were not the monolith among Blacks that we have been told.

Indeed, the Black American who finds themself to be theologically conservative, prizing civic order and household stability over agitation, is not abandoning their race in the least bit but picking an old and well-attested line that well predates them.

Long before modern debates about race, activism, and social justice, many Black Christian leaders warned that a movement built upon protest rather than production would eventually weaken the institutions that sustained our communities. Their concerns were not rooted in indifference to injustice, but in a conviction that the Church's first mission was spiritual rather than political.

The clearest example came in 1961 with the schism between the National Baptist Convention and the newly formed Progressive National Baptist Convention.

The split was not merely administrative. It reflected a fundamental disagreement about the nature of Christian faithfulness in public life.

On one side stood Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. and ministers who believed civil disobedience, demonstrations, and organized protest should become central instruments of social change. Their efforts would eventually shape the public image of the Black church for generations to come.

On the other side stood Dr. Joseph H. Jackson and the leadership of the National Baptist Convention. Jackson opposed segregation and supported equal rights under the law, but he believed that civil disobedience and public disorder would ultimately damage both the church and the communities it hoped to serve.

Jackson argued that the Church's primary task was conversion, discipleship, sanctification, family formation, education, institution-building, and economic responsibility. He believed that Christians should pursue justice through lawful means while simultaneously strengthening the moral and economic foundations of their communities.

This conviction was not merely theoretical.

Under Jackson's leadership, the National Baptist Convention invested heavily in publishing, education, missions, and economic development. The Convention established agricultural projects to assist displaced Black families. It pursued land development initiatives. Jackson repeatedly called on Black Christians to move "from protest to production," arguing that ownership, thrift, education, and enterprise would accomplish more than perpetual agitation.

Whether one agrees with Jackson's conclusions or not, it is impossible to deny that he represented a substantial tradition within Black American Christianity.

That tradition stretched backward through leaders such as E. C. Morris, Booker T. Washington, and countless pastors, educators, and church builders who believed that strong families, faithful churches, disciplined lives, and productive institutions were the surest path to human flourishing.

Today that tradition is often forgotten.

Many assume that opposition to protest politics represents a departure from Black history. In reality, it represents a return to one of its oldest and most influential streams.

The question facing Christians today is not whether injustice exists. The question is whether the remedies that dominate our public imagination are the same remedies our forefathers believed would produce lasting fruit.

The National Baptist leaders who opposed the activist turn of the 1960s believed that social renewal begins with redeemed hearts, disciplined households, faithful churches, sound doctrine, productive labor, and peaceful relations among neighbors.

Their answer was not riot, resentment, or revolution. It was Christian formation. More than sixty years later, their challenge remains before us and their heritage is available to us.

They prayed for their posterity that we would pursue Christ and see the fruit of His grace and kindness in our lives and homes. So don't let naysayers or your own internal fears make you think you're abandoning your heritage by loving Christ, being theologically faithful, and seeking peace rather than agitation in the public square. If you're doing these things, you are in line with a rich heritage that will not fail to be fruitful.
